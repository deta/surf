#### **Slide 3: Introduction to Hypocalcemia**
**Duration**: 3 minutes

**Key Points**:
- Definition: Total calcium <8.5 mg/dL or ionized <4.5 mg/dL
- Higher risk in pediatrics due to growth demands
- Common in NICU (10-20% of critically ill children)

**Speaker Notes**:
> "Hypocalcemia is defined by low serum calcium levels. In pediatrics, this is particularly concerning because calcium is essential for bone development, neuromuscular function, and cardiac rhythm. Children have higher calcium demands than adults, making them more vulnerable to deficiency. The prevalence is especially high in NICU settings where we see rates of 10-20% in critically ill infants."

**Clinical Pearl**: Emphasize that ionized calcium is the physiologically active form.

---

#### **Slide 4: Pathophysiology**
**Duration**: 4 minutes

**Key Points**:
- Calcium homeostasis involves PTH, Vitamin D, and calcitonin
- Pediatric kidneys and bones are immature
- Low calcium → hyperexcitable nerves/muscles

**Speaker Notes**:
> "Understanding calcium homeostasis is crucial. The parathyroid gland senses low calcium and releases PTH, which activates vitamin D and promotes calcium absorption from the gut and reabsorption from the kidneys. In children, immature organ systems make this balance more fragile. When calcium drops, nerve and muscle cells become hyperexcitable, leading to tetany, seizures, and potentially fatal arrhythmias."

**Teaching Point**: Use the flowchart to walk through the feedback loop.

---

#### **Slide 5: Causes in Pediatric Patients**
**Duration**: 4 minutes

**Key Points**:
- Five main categories: Nutritional, Endocrine, Renal, Iatrogenic, Neonatal
- Vitamin D deficiency is most common
- Consider developmental factors

**Speaker Notes**:
> "Pediatric causes differ significantly from adults. Nutritional deficiencies, particularly vitamin D, are most common. We also see endocrine disorders like hypoparathyroidism, often from DiGeorge syndrome. Renal causes include chronic kidney disease. Iatrogenic causes include medications like anticonvulsants. In neonates, prematurity and maternal diabetes are key risk factors."

**Clinical Pearl**: Always ask about vitamin D supplementation and sun exposure.

---

#### **Slide 6: Clinical Presentation**
**Duration**: 4 minutes

**Key Points**:
- Symptoms vary by system and age
- Neuromuscular: Tetany, seizures (most dramatic)
- Cardiovascular: QT prolongation (life-threatening)
- Infants may present subtly (irritability, poor feeding)

**Speaker Notes**:
> "Clinical presentation varies widely. Neuromuscular symptoms include tetany—look for Chvostek's and Trousseau's signs—muscle cramps, and seizures. Cardiovascular effects include prolonged QT interval, which can lead to fatal arrhythmias. Skeletal manifestations include rickets and fractures. In infants, symptoms may be subtle: irritability, poor feeding, or jitteriness. Severe cases can present with laryngospasm or apnea."

**Clinical Pearl**: Always check ECG for QT prolongation in suspected cases.

---

#### **Slide 7: Diagnosis**
**Duration**: 3 minutes

**Key Points**:
- Ionized calcium is gold standard
- Comprehensive workup: PTH, Vitamin D, phosphate, magnesium
- ECG for QT interval
- X-rays for rickets

**Speaker Notes**:
> "Diagnosis starts with measuring calcium levels. Ionized calcium is the gold standard because total calcium can be misleading due to albumin binding. A comprehensive workup includes PTH, vitamin D levels, phosphate, and magnesium—which is essential for PTH secretion. Always get an ECG to assess QT interval. In chronic cases, X-rays may reveal rickets with characteristic bowing of long bones."

**Important**: Emphasize that albumin levels affect total calcium interpretation.

---

#### **Slide 8: Management and Treatment**
**Duration**: 5 minutes

**Key Points**:
- Acute: IV calcium gluconate (10-20 mg/kg over 10-30 min)
- Monitor cardiac rhythm during infusion
- Chronic: Oral calcium + Vitamin D
- Always treat underlying cause

**Speaker Notes**:
> "Management is divided into acute and chronic phases. For acute symptomatic hypocalcemia, give IV calcium gluconate 10-20 mg/kg over 10-30 minutes with continuous cardiac monitoring. Address life-threatening complications first. Don't forget to check and correct magnesium—it's essential for PTH secretion. For chronic management, use oral calcium supplements 30-75 mg/kg/day in divided doses, plus vitamin D—typically calcitriol. Most importantly, identify and treat the underlying cause."

**Safety Note**: Never give IV calcium rapidly—risk of bradycardia and cardiac arrest.

---

#### **Slide 9: Complications & Prevention**
**Duration**: 3 minutes

**Key Points**:
- Short-term: Seizures, cardiac arrest, laryngospasm
- Long-term: Developmental delays, osteoporosis
- Prevention: Vitamin D supplementation (400 IU/day for infants)

**Speaker Notes**:
> "Complications can be severe. Short-term risks include seizures, cardiac arrest, and respiratory failure from laryngospasm. Long-term untreated hypocalcemia can cause developmental delays, osteoporosis, and dental problems. Prevention is straightforward: ensure adequate vitamin D supplementation—400 IU daily for infants—promote breastfeeding, and screen high-risk populations like preterm infants and babies of diabetic mothers."

**Key Message**: Early recognition and treatment prevent serious complications.

---

#### **Slide 10: Case Study**
**Duration**: 5 minutes

**Key Points**:
- 6-month-old with seizures
- Exclusively breastfed, no vitamin D supplementation
- Calcium 6.5 mg/dL, low vitamin D
- Successful treatment with IV calcium + vitamin D

**Speaker Notes**:
> "Let's apply this to a real case. A 6-month-old infant presents with seizures. History reveals exclusive breastfeeding with no vitamin D supplementation and limited sun exposure. Physical exam shows irritability and positive Chvostek's sign. Labs show calcium of 6.5 mg/dL and vitamin D of only 8 ng/mL. This is classic vitamin D deficiency hypocalcemia. We treated with IV calcium for acute stabilization, then started oral vitamin D supplementation. The infant recovered fully within 48 hours and continues on maintenance vitamin D."

**Discussion Point**: Ask audience about differential diagnoses.

---

#### **Slide 11: Key Takeaways**
**Duration**: 3 minutes

**Key Points**:
- Early recognition is critical
- Measure ionized calcium
- Tailor treatment to acute vs chronic
- Prevention through supplementation
- Multidisciplinary approach

**Speaker Notes**:
> "Let me summarize five key takeaways. First, maintain a high index of suspicion in at-risk populations—early recognition saves lives. Second, always measure ionized calcium for accurate diagnosis. Third, tailor treatment: IV calcium for acute symptomatic cases, oral supplements for chronic management. Fourth, prevention through vitamin D supplementation is highly effective. Finally, optimal care requires collaboration between pediatrics, endocrinology, nutrition, and family education."

**Emphasis**: These five points should guide your clinical practice.

## Common Questions & Answers

**Q: When should I consult pediatric endocrinology?**
A: Consult for recurrent hypocalcemia, suspected hypoparathyroidism, pseudohypoparathyroidism, or cases not responding to standard treatment.

**Q: How long should vitamin D supplementation continue?**
A: For deficiency, treat until levels normalize (typically 8-12 weeks), then maintain with 400 IU/day. For chronic conditions, lifelong supplementation may be needed.

**Q: What's the difference between calcium gluconate and calcium chloride?**
A: Calcium gluconate is preferred in pediatrics due to lower risk of tissue necrosis if extravasation occurs. Calcium chloride has more elemental calcium but is more caustic.

**Q: Can you give oral calcium during acute treatment?**
A: For symptomatic hypocalcemia, IV calcium is required for rapid correction. Oral calcium is too slow for acute management.

**Q: How do you monitor for hypercalcemia during treatment?**
A: Check calcium levels 24-48 hours after starting treatment, then weekly until stable. Watch for symptoms: constipation, polyuria, lethargy.
